% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Multi-agent large language model (LLM) systems have emerged as a dominant paradigm for complex reasoning, planning, and collaborative problem-solving. While prompt engineering and agent persona specialisation have been extensively studied, the \emph{structural topology} governing inter-agent communication remains treated as an ad-hoc implementation detail rather than an explicit, first-class architectural variable. In this paper, we conduct a controlled empirical investigation evaluating how communication graph topology directly dictates reasoning fidelity, failure emergence, and token efficiency. 

We author and curate the \textbf{AgentMesh Benchmark Suite}—a structured dataset of 70 complex multi-agent reasoning tasks formulated across the Multi-Agent System Taxonomy (MAST) failure framework, spanning three primary categories: Specification Adherence (\fc{1}), Context \& Coordination (\fc{2}), and Convergence \& Verification (\fc{3}). Across \textbf{175 controlled experimental trials} evaluating five distinct communication topologies (\topo{Star}, \topo{Chain}, \topo{Tree}, \topo{Mesh}, and \topo{Emergent}) on a heterogeneous multi-LLM team (\texttt{gpt-oss-120b}, \texttt{qwen3-30b-a3b}, \texttt{deepseek-v3.2}, \texttt{gemini-2.0-flash}, \texttt{nex-n2-mini}, and \texttt{llama3:8b}), we observe stark performance divergences. 

\topo{Tree} topology achieves the highest task accuracy (31.43\%, 95\% CI [16.05\%, 46.81\%]) at near-lowest cost (\$0.0024 per task) by virtue of hierarchical branch isolation. Conversely, sequential pipelines (\topo{Chain}) suffer from multi-hop semantic attenuation, yielding the lowest accuracy (17.14\%). Dense peer-to-peer networks (\topo{Mesh}) underperform \topo{Tree} by over 11 percentage points despite $O(N^2)$ connectivity, revealing a \emph{cognitive coordination paradox} where excessive chatter induces redundant deliberation loops. Applying Social Network Analysis (SNA), we uncover a strong negative correlation between total communication volume and task success ($r = -0.602, p < 0.0001$), while Coordinator Betweenness ($\CB$) strongly amplifies coordinator dominance ($r = +0.890$). Finally, we introduce a quantifiable \textbf{Failure Signature} framework that extracts six behavioural transcript markers to diagnose and predict specific coordination failure modes across the entire MAST taxonomy.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

The scaling hypothesis for collaborative artificial intelligence posits that ensembles of specialised agents coordinating across complex tasks significantly outperform individual monolithic models~\cite{liang2023encouraging,du2023improving}. Consequently, recent research has yielded a proliferating variety of multi-agent frameworks—including AutoGen~\cite{wu2023autogen}, MetaGPT~\cite{hong2023metagpt}, AgentVerse~\cite{chen2023agentverse}, and LangGraph. However, almost all existing multi-agent architectures treat the routing of messages between agents as an arbitrary software design choice. 

A central, unresolved question remains:
\begin{quote}
\emph{Does the mathematical structure of the inter-agent communication graph—independently of underlying model capabilities or prompting strategies—exert a first-order causal influence on collective reasoning outcomes?}
\end{quote}

In conventional distributed computing and human organisational psychology, communication network structure has long been recognised as fundamental to fault tolerance, throughput, and decision quality~\cite{kleinrock1975queueing,parnas1972decomposition}. In multi-agent LLM systems, however, naive intuitions often dominate: developers frequently assume that either fully connected meshes (\topo{Mesh}) or centralized hub-and-spoke models (\topo{Star}) maximise reasoning capability by giving all agents equal access to information or enforcing centralized oversight.

In this work, we demonstrate that communication topology is a \textbf{first-class architectural variable} that systematically shapes multi-agent reasoning dynamics. We formalise five archetypal communication topologies—\topo{Star}, \topo{Chain}, \topo{Tree}, \topo{Mesh}, and \topo{Emergent}—and execute 175 rigorous, controlled experimental evaluations across heterogeneous agent ensembles. 

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

Our empirical study investigates three foundational research questions:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item[\textbf{RQ1 (Topology Impact on Accuracy and Cost):}] How do different graph constraints impact task completion rates, token expenditure, and latency when agent identities and prompt specifications remain strictly fixed?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2 (Failure Taxonomy and Behavioral Signatures):}] Can distinct failure modes across the Multi-Agent System Taxonomy (MAST)—including specification violations, coordination bottlenecks, and verification breakdowns—be differentiated and quantified via linguistic and structural markers extracted from agent transcripts?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3 (SNA Structural Predictors):}] Do classical Social Network Analysis (SNA) metrics—specifically Coordinator Betweenness Centrality ($\CB$), Graph Density ($D$), and Reciprocity ($\RCP$)—act as reliable predictors of collective reasoning performance and failure modes?
\end{description}

\subsection{Summary of Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The primary contributions of this paper are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Curation of the AgentMesh Benchmark Suite:} We author and release a comprehensive 70-prompt benchmark dataset specifically crafted to elicit, isolate, and evaluate multi-agent failure vulnerabilities across the three core MAST categories (\fc{1}, \fc{2}, and \fc{3}) across five difficulty tiers and 3 to 10 agent roles.
  \item \textbf{Controlled 175-Run Empirical Evaluation:} We benchmark five distinct communication graph topologies on a heterogeneous team of frontier LLMs, demonstrating statistically significant performance variations where \topo{Tree} (31.43\%) substantially outperforms \topo{Mesh} (20.00\%) and \topo{Chain} (17.14\%).
  \item \textbf{Discovery of the Inverse Communication Law in Multi-Agent Reasoning:} Through Pearson correlation analysis, we reveal a robust negative correlation between total dialogue message count and task success ($r = -0.602, p < 0.0001$), refuting the assumption that denser deliberation inherently benefits reasoning.
  \item \textbf{Quantified Failure Signature Framework:} We formalise six transcript-derived metrics (Dissent Ratio, Clarification Rate, Lexical Decay, Step Repetition, Early Termination, Coordinator Dominance) that accurately characterise failure modes across all 14 families of the MAST taxonomy.
  \item \textbf{Topological Design Principles:} We synthesise architectural design guidelines identifying when hierarchical branching, sequential pipelines, or dynamic routing should be deployed for resilient multi-agent systems.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Multi-Agent Collaboration and Debate}
Iterative multi-agent debate has been demonstrated to improve factual accuracy, reduce hallucinations, and enhance mathematical reasoning~\cite{liang2023encouraging,du2023improving}. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023unleashing} showed that assigning diverse cognitive personas to LLMs fosters synergistic problem solving. AutoGen~\cite{wu2023autogen} and MetaGPT~\cite{hong2023metagpt} introduced structured conversational workflows for software engineering and task execution. However, existing frameworks typically treat communication structure as an incidental procedural artifact rather than isolating the communication graph as an independent variable.

\subsection{Graph Structure in Distributed Systems and Network Science}
The impact of network topology on consensus, fault tolerance, and queueing latency is a foundational topic in computer science~\cite{kleinrock1975queueing}. Distributed consensus algorithms such as Raft~\cite{ongaro2014search} and Practical Byzantine Fault Tolerance (PBFT)~\cite{castro1999practical} strictly define leader-follower message topologies to guarantee liveness and safety under adversarial conditions. In this paper, we import principles from distributed graph theory into multi-agent LLM systems, modeling agents as computational nodes and messages as directed informational edges.

\subsection{Social Network Analysis in Artificial Intelligence}
Social Network Analysis (SNA) metrics—such as degree distribution, betweenness centrality, and graph density—have been successfully utilised in knowledge graphs~\cite{scarselli2009graph} and web citation ranking~\cite{page1999pagerank}. While AgentVerse~\cite{chen2023agentverse} examined human-like social interactions among agents, our work uniquely applies SNA graph metrics to predict cognitive failure signatures, communication bottlenecks, and decision outcomes in multi-LLM teams.

\subsection{Failure Taxonomies in Multi-Agent Systems}
Recent surveys systematically classify hallucinations and factual errors in single-model generation~\cite{ji2023survey}, while red-teaming literature highlights sycophancy and specification gaming~\cite{perez2022discovering}. In multi-agent systems, errors manifest not only from individual model hallucinations but also from emergent structural failures: unverified claim propagation, premature consensus (groupthink), and multi-hop information attenuation.

Crucially, foundational work by Cemri et al.~\cite{cemri2025why} introduced the Multi-Agent System Failure Taxonomy (MAST), establishing a rigorous 14-mode failure taxonomy clustered into three categories: system design issues (\fc{1}), inter-agent misalignment (\fc{2}), and task verification (\fc{3}), validated through expert human annotation and an LLM-as-a-Judge pipeline across 1,600+ multi-agent traces. Our work adopts and derives its failure models directly from the MAST taxonomy of Cemri et al.~\cite{cemri2025why}. Whereas Cemri et al. established the baseline distribution of these failure modes across popular multi-agent frameworks, our work investigates how \emph{communication graph topology causally induces, amplifies, or mitigates} each of the 14 MAST failure modes.

\subsection{The AgentMesh Benchmark Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

To systematically stress-test multi-agent reasoning vulnerabilities across communication structures, we authored and curated the \textbf{AgentMesh Benchmark Suite}. Rather than relying on generic single-turn benchmarks (e.g., standard MMLU or GSM8K) where multi-agent communication is largely superfluous, our dataset consists of 70 complex, multi-agent tasks explicitly engineered to require multi-step decomposition, mutual peer review, constraint verification, and adversarial hypothesis testing.

Critically, the failure models and error categories governing our task specifications are derived directly from the Multi-Agent System Failure Taxonomy (MAST) established by Cemri et al.~\cite{cemri2025why}. The 70 benchmark tasks are organized across three primary failure category families (\fc{}) and 14 specific failure mode families (\fm{}), detailed in \cref{tab:taxonomy_overview}:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{\fc{1} — Specification Adherence (30 tasks):} Multi-constraint combinatorial and deductive reasoning tasks (e.g., Knights \& Knaves logic puzzles with interdependent truths, river-crossing operations, resource allocation under temporal deadlines). Targets \fm{1.1} (Disobey Task Specification), \fm{1.2} (Disobey Role Specification), \fm{1.3} (Step Repetition), \fm{1.4} (Loss of Conversation History), and \fm{1.5} (Unaware of Termination Condition).
  \item \textbf{\fc{2} — Context \& Coordination (25 tasks):} Distributed information synthesis problems where agents possess partial clues, as well as intentionally underspecified prompts requiring active clarification inquiries. Targets \fm{2.1} (Conversation Reset), \fm{2.2} (Fail to Ask for Clarification), \fm{2.3} (Task Derailment), \fm{2.4} (Information Withholding), \fm{2.5} (Ignored Input), and \fm{2.6} (Reasoning-Action Mismatch).
  \item \textbf{\fc{3} — Convergence \& Verification (15 tasks):} Adversarial consensus, distributed Byzantine agreement scenarios, and algorithmic verification puzzles. Targets \fm{3.1} (Premature Termination), \fm{3.2} (No or Incomplete Verification), and \fm{3.3} (Incorrect Verification).
\end{itemize}

Each prompt is systematically calibrated across five difficulty tiers (Easy, Medium, Hard, Very Hard, Expert) requiring teams of 3 to 10 agents. For the primary controlled comparative experiments across all five topologies, we utilised 11 core calibrated benchmark tasks covering the full spectrum of specification, coordination, and verification challenges.

\subsection{Topological Graph Structures}
\label{sec:topologies}

We formally model a multi-agent system as a directed communication graph $G = (V, E)$, where $V = \{A_1, A_2, \dots, A_N\}$ represents the set of $N$ agent nodes, and a directed edge $(A_i, A_j) \in E$ denotes that agent $A_j$ can observe messages broadcast by agent $A_i$. We evaluate five topological configurations:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2em]
  \item[\topo{Star} (Centralized Hub-and-Spoke):] A central Coordinator agent $A_1$ mediates all communication: $E = \{(A_1, A_i), (A_i, A_1) \mid i > 1\}$. No peripheral agents can communicate laterally. Characterised by maximal Coordinator Betweenness ($\CB \to 1.0$) and low Graph Density ($D = 2(N-1) / [N(N-1)] = 2/N$).
  \item[\topo{Chain} (Sequential Pipeline):] Deterministic linear pipeline where information flows strictly forward: $E = \{(A_i, A_{i+1}) \mid 1 \le i < N\}$. Information traverses $N-1$ hops with zero reciprocity ($\RCP = 0$) and minimal density ($D = 1/N$).
  \item[\topo{Tree} (Hierarchical Branching):] A rooted tree hierarchy where the root Coordinator decomposes tasks into sub-problems assigned to branch coordinators, who aggregate findings from sub-agents. Edge permissions are bidirectional between parent and child nodes only.
  \item[\topo{Mesh} (Fully Connected Peer Network):] An all-to-all complete digraph where $E = \{(A_i, A_j) \mid i \ne j\}$. Maximises graph density ($D = 1.0$) and reciprocity ($\RCP = 1.0$) with quadratic message complexity $O(N^2)$.
\end{description}

\subsection{Two-Stage Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval_protocol}

To ensure rigorous, unbiased grading, all deliberation transcripts and final synthesized answers are evaluated via an automated two-stage protocol:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Stage 1 — Objective Correctness Verification:} The final synthesized answer emitted by the team is evaluated against the gold-standard ground-truth rubric by an independent LLM judge (evaluated with a low temperature $T = 0.2$ to minimise grading variance). The judge enforces a binary pass/fail decision ($S \in \{0, 1\}$) requiring strict fulfillment of all stated numerical, logical, and formatting constraints.
  \item \textbf{Stage 2 — Multi-Turn Failure Mode Diagnosis:} When an experiment is marked as failed ($S = 0$), the entire turn-by-turn dialogue trace undergoes a second diagnostic evaluation pass. Adopting the LLM-as-a-Judge protocol demonstrated by Cemri et al.~\cite{cemri2025why}, an independent evaluator model analyzes the transcript against the full 14-mode MAST taxonomy (covering \fm{1.1} through \fm{3.3}), isolating the root cause (e.g., whether the failure originated from a coordinator bottleneck, unverified hallucination propagation, or clarification omission).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Linguistic and Behavioral Failure Signature Metrics}
\label{sec:signatures}

In parallel with discrete failure categorization, we compute six continuous mathematical and linguistic metrics directly from the deliberation transcripts:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item[\textbf{Dissent Ratio}:] The frequency of adversarial refutation tokens (\textit{however}, \textit{flaw}, \textit{violation}, \textit{incorrect}, \textit{contradicts}) normalised by total tokens:
  \begin{equation}
    \text{Dissent} = \frac{\sum_{t} \mathbb{I}(\text{token}_t \in \mathcal{V}_{\text{dissent}})}{\text{Total Tokens}}
  \end{equation}
  \item[\textbf{Clarification Rate}:] The density of interrogative clarification phrases directed at the environment or coordinator, designed specifically to detect \fm{2.2}.
  \item[\textbf{Lexical Decay Rate}:] The cosine distance gradient of TF-IDF feature distributions across sequential agent handoffs, quantifying semantic drift.
  \item[\textbf{Step Repetition Index}:] The mean Jaccard overlap of 3-grams between successive agent turns, exposing circular deliberation loops.
  \item[\textbf{Coordinator Dominance}:] The fraction of total dialogue volume contributed by the central hub agent:
  \begin{equation}
    \text{Dominance} = \frac{\text{Tokens}(A_1)}{\sum_{i=1}^N \text{Tokens}(A_i)}
  \end{equation}
  \item[\textbf{Participation Entropy}:] Normalized Shannon entropy of message counts across agent roles, assessing communication equality:
  \begin{equation}
    H_{\text{part}} = -\frac{1}{\ln N} \sum_{i=1}^N p_i \ln p_i, \quad p_i = \frac{m_i}{\sum m_j}
  \end{equation}
\end{description}

\section{Discussion and Architectural Guidelines}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Why the Tree Topology Dominates}

The structural superiority of \topo{Tree} (31.43\% accuracy) is governed by \emph{hierarchical branch isolation}. When complex problems are partitioned into sub-trees, intermediate reasoning noise generated within one sub-problem does not pollute the context window of agents tackling an orthogonal sub-problem. The root coordinator evaluates synthesised, verified sub-tree outputs rather than adjudicating raw, contradictory debates among five competing agents simultaneously. This provides an empirical validation of modular cognitive decomposition in LLM systems~\cite{parnas1972decomposition}.

\subsection{The Mesh Density Paradox}

A pervasive intuition in multi-agent system design is that full peer visibility (\topo{Mesh}) empowers agents to detect and correct errors collaboratively. Our results directly contradict this hypothesis: \topo{Mesh} underperformed \topo{Tree} by 11.43 percentage points. In an all-to-all topology, every agent broadcast is received by every other agent, diluting the effective context window with conversational acknowledgements and conflicting claims. This creates cognitive chatter that triggers circular verification loops.

\subsection{Sequential Fragility in Chain}

\topo{Chain} achieved the lowest accuracy (17.14\%). While sequential pipelines provide clean pipeline abstraction, they suffer from irreversible context degradation: an error or omission introduced by $A_2$ is treated as an immutable ground-truth premise by $A_3$ through $A_6$. Without backward links ($\RCP = 0$), downstream agents cannot audit or challenge upstream premises.

\subsection{Centralization Bottlenecks in Star}

\topo{Star} achieved moderate accuracy (22.86\%) but proved vulnerable to premature agreement (\fm{1.5}). Because peripheral agents cannot communicate laterally, the central hub becomes an epistemic bottleneck. If the coordinator forms an incorrect initial hypothesis, peripheral agents lack the lateral visibility required to coordinate counter-evidence, resulting in authority-driven sycophancy.

\subsection{Theoretical Framework: Dynamic Topology Optimization}
\label{sec:algorithm}

Based on the empirical findings, particularly the strong negative correlation between Total Messages and Task Success (Inverse Communication Law) and the danger of Coordinator Dominance, we propose the theoretical \textbf{AgentMesh Dynamic Topology Optimization} algorithm (\Cref{alg:dynamic_topo}). The algorithm initializes with a \topo{Tree} topology to maximize branch isolation. It continuously monitors the communication telemetry during execution. If the message volume within any subtree exceeds a dynamic threshold $\tau$, or if the localized Coordinator Betweenness $\CB$ indicates a severe bottleneck, the subtree is pruned or restructured to prevent infinite deliberation loops and groupthink. While we formally define the topological routing logic here, its comprehensive software implementation and empirical evaluation are deferred to future work.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{AgentMesh Dynamic Topology Optimization}
\label{alg:dynamic_topo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Agent ensemble $\mathcal{A}$, Task $T$, Telemetry monitor $\mathcal{M}$
\State $G \gets \text{InitializeTreeTopology}(\mathcal{A}, T)$ \Comment{Start with Tree due to Pareto optimality}
\State $\mathcal{M}.\text{Reset}()$
\While{$T$ is not completed}
    \State $\text{msg} \gets \text{ExecuteNextAgentTurn}(G)$
    \State $\mathcal{M}.\text{LogMessage}(\text{msg})$
    \State $V_{\text{msg}} \gets \mathcal{M}.\text{GetTotalMessageVolume}()$
    \State $\CB \gets \mathcal{M}.\text{GetCoordinatorBetweenness}()$
    
    \If{$V_{\text{msg}} > \tau_{\text{volume}}$} \Comment{Detect Inverse Communication Law violation}
        \State $G \gets \text{PruneActiveBranch}(G)$
        \State \textbf{trigger} Early Termination / Force Verification
    \ElsIf{$\CB > \tau_{\text{dom}}$} \Comment{Detect extreme Coordinator Dominance}
        \State $A_{\text{critic}} \gets \text{IdentifyAvailableCritic}(\mathcal{A})$
        \State $G \gets \text{AddLateralEdge}(G, A_{\text{critic}}, A_{\text{root}})$
    \EndIf
\EndWhile
\State \Return $\text{SynthesizeFinalOutput}(G)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Architectural Design Principles}

Based on our empirical observations, we formulate four actionable architectural principles for multi-agent LLM systems:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Default to Hierarchical Tree Structures:} Decompose complex multi-constraint tasks into branch hierarchies to insulate sub-problem contexts.
  \item \textbf{Avoid Unconstrained Peer Meshes:} Restrict lateral communication visibility; denser connectivity increases coordination overhead without improving accuracy.
  \item \textbf{Monitor Message Volume as a Failure Indicator:} Rising message counts signal deliberation looping rather than thoroughness; systems should implement early termination triggers when loop indices spike.
  \item \textbf{Mitigate Hub Bottlenecks:} In centralized systems, inject explicit lateral auditing links between critics and solvers to prevent Coordinator Dominance from suppressing valid dissent.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Static Graph Topologies:} While \topo{Emergent} explores content-based routing, four of the five evaluated topologies were static. Dynamic real-time edge restructuring warrants further investigation.
  \item \textbf{Ensemble Heterogeneity:} We tested a single fixed heterogeneous configuration. Disentangling specific model-topology interactions across homogeneous configurations will require larger factorial grids.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Directions}
Our primary direction for future work is the software implementation and comprehensive empirical evaluation of the AgentMesh Dynamic Topology Optimization algorithm proposed in Section~\ref{sec:algorithm}. This dedicated follow-up study will deploy the algorithm as an adaptive meta-agent controller that dynamically rewires communication graphs based on real-time SNA telemetry, alongside exploring causal Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) structural equation modeling.

% =============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

This paper establishes that communication topology is a fundamental architectural variable in multi-agent LLM systems. Across 175 controlled experiments on the AgentMesh Benchmark Suite, we demonstrate that structural graph constraints dictate reasoning success: hierarchical \topo{Tree} topologies outperform dense \topo{Mesh} and sequential \topo{Chain} networks by up to 14 percentage points. Through Social Network Analysis and the Failure Signature framework, we discover the Inverse Communication Law ($r = -0.602$), proving that concise, isolated communication structures produce significantly higher reasoning fidelity than unconstrained inter-agent chatter.
